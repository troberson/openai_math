## contents

**197. A torsion-free group algebra that is not directly finite.** Constructs a finitely presented torsion-free nonsofic group whose group algebra over 𝔽_2 is not directly finite, disproving Kaplansky's conjecture even without torsion. Companion examples give injective nonsurjective cellular automata on all configurations, refuting Gottschalk's surjunctivity conjecture. Another counterexample is an integral group-ring matrix, invertible over the rational group ring, with Fuglede–Kadison determinant strictly between zero and one, disproving the unrestricted Determinant Conjecture. ([Lean](../../lean/docs/197.md))

The blurb above is reproduced from [S1:4518]; the release renders the two-element field with subscript markup and this page writes it as 𝔽_2.

## undergraduate

A group algebra is built from a field and a group by taking finite formal sums of group elements with coefficients in that field, multiplying the way the group law says to. It is a ring, so it has a multiplicative identity, and one can ask the familiar matrix question: if two elements multiply to 1 in one order, must they also multiply to 1 in the reversed order? A ring with that property is called *directly finite*. For matrices over a field the answer is yes, because finite dimension forces it. Kaplansky conjectured that the finiteness of the support of each group-algebra element — every sum mentions only finitely many group elements — plays the same role for every field and every group, and the release states the question as asking “{{1}}” [S9:4].

Family 197 answers that question three times, in three different settings, and each answer is a counterexample. The manuscript the headline names, dated “{{2}}” [S2:4], produces a group with no element of finite order, given by finitely many generators and relations, whose group algebra over the two-element field contains elements *a*, *b*, *c* with *ab* = 1, *ac* = 0, and *c* nonzero [S4:14–17]. The extra element *c* is the whole point: if *ba* were 1, then *c* = (*ba*)*c* = *b*(*ac*) = 0, contradicting *c* ≠ 0 [S4:23–24]. The other two manuscripts, dated “{{3}}” [S7:5] [S14:5] and “{{4}}” [S17:5], produce counterexamples in characteristic two using a group that does have torsion [S9:8–16] and in odd characteristic at one specific prime [S19:13–22].

Two consequences travel with the algebra, and both are statements about groups rather than about rings. A group is *sofic* if its multiplication can be approximated by permutations of large finite sets; a theorem of Elek and Szabó says group algebras of sofic groups are directly finite, so the groups constructed here cannot be sofic [S4:39–40]. A group is *surjunctive* if every injective cellular automaton on its full configuration space is surjective, which is Gottschalk's conjecture that every group is. The coefficients that witness *ab* = 1 ≠ *ba* define a cellular automaton that is injective and not surjective, so the groups here are not surjunctive either [S12:134–141].

**Proof sketch.** The October 4 manuscript starts from a bouquet of circles and attaches *cones* on finite labeled graphs: a cone kills every closed path in its graph, so a vertex determines a group element by reading a path from a fixed root [S4:67–71]. The algebra elements are finite sums of these path labels and their inverses. Multiplying two such sums means summing over pairs of paths, and the summands organize into components on which the contribution is constant; a component whose vertices all have odd degree has even size, so its contributions cancel over 𝔽_2, while a single even-degree vertex leaves exactly one contribution standing [S4:73–80]. Seven extra labels, arranged by the complements of lines in the Fano plane, create that one exception in the product *ab* and no exception in the product *ac* [S4:83–86]. The hard part is group-theoretic rather than arithmetic: the cone attachments must be aspherical, and paths from a root to different vertices must stay distinct in the group, which is proved by excluding planar pairings of graph paths [S4:88–101]. The final assembly chooses one finite random graph outcome with the required properties and reads off *a*, *b*, *c* from the parity calculation [S5:4–23].

## history

The manuscripts date the conjecture to Kaplansky's own formulation, which the October 4 manuscript cites as a problem in Part II of Kaplansky 1972 [S4:27–29]. The characteristic-zero case is older than the family and is settled: group algebras are directly finite for every group in characteristic zero, by an operator-algebra argument placing $\mathbb C[G]$ in a finite tracial setting, with Montgomery's proof via the operator-norm closure of the regular representation extending it to every finite matrix size [S4:29–31] [S9:21]. The positive-characteristic question is where the family sits, and the manuscripts list the standing partial results as constraints on any counterexample rather than as properties of theirs: Ara, O'Meara, and Perera proved stable finiteness for free-by-amenable groups over division rings [S4:35–37], Elek and Szabó extended the conclusion to sofic groups [S4:37–40], and the characteristic-two manuscript is explicit that “{{49}}” [S9:23]. Dykema, Heister, and Juschenko organized one-sided-inverse cancellation patterns into universal groups and proved direct finiteness for small support pairs over 𝔽_2 [S4:41–44]; Dykema and Juschenko's reduction, which the characteristic-two manuscript realizes explicitly, turns stable finiteness into direct finiteness after taking the direct product with every finite group [S9:25].

The dynamical half has its own lineage. Gottschalk's surjunctivity conjecture dates to 1973 [S9:47]; Gromov's symbolic-algebraic surjunctivity theorem and Weiss's finite-alphabet sofic theorem cover sofic groups, and the linear theory of Ceccherini-Silberstein and Coornaert quantifies the equivalence between stable finiteness and injective-to-surjective behavior of linear cellular automata, with Phung proving stable finiteness over every field for surjunctive groups [S9:49]. The odd-characteristic manuscript gives the same lineage [S19:147–159].

The determinant conjecture's lineage is the $L^2$-invariant theory: Lück's conjecture, Schick's bound for amenable groups, Elek and Szabó's proof for sofic groups using finite graph approximations and integrality of products of nonzero eigenvalues of positive integral matrices, and the Fuglede–Kadison determinant as the operator-theoretic object the conjecture bounds [S15:124–152]. The manuscript positions its method against that lineage: “{{50}}” [S15:131–132].

Prior (my training data): Kaplansky's three finiteness conjectures — direct finiteness, the zero-divisor property, and unit conjecture — were the standard open problems in this area, with the characteristic-zero cases long settled and positive characteristic open in general; the soficity and surjunctivity conjectures were open in the same period, and Elek–Szabó and Gromov–Weiss were the known partial results. This is my prior, not a claim sourced from the release.

## consequences

The family settles the unrestricted conjecture negatively, and it does so in a way that removes the standard escape routes. Torsion is not needed: the headline result is torsion-free, so the counterexample survives the strongest structural restriction in the literature. The field is not the escape route either — the headline result uses the prime field 𝔽_2, and the characteristic-two companion uses a finite field of characteristic two that is “{{51}}” [S15:118–119]. What remains open is the odd-characteristic case in general: that manuscript's own scope line is “{{33}}” [S19:28–29].

Three named conjectures fall. Direct finiteness fails, soficity is not universal — the characteristic-two manuscript supplies “{{53}}” [S9:40] — and Gottschalk's surjunctivity conjecture is refuted twice inside the family, once for the characteristic-two group [S12:134–141] and once for the odd-characteristic group [S20:282–283]. The characteristic-two manuscript also transports its counterexample outward: a separate embedding theorem moves the same scalar pair into an overgroup of type $F_\infty$, whose conclusion is “{{54}}” [S12:171], so the counterexamples are not confined to the groups the construction names.

The determinant consequence is the one with a formal dependency chain inside the release. The determinant counterexample is derived from the characteristic-two scalar pair, in the manuscript [S15:107–112] and in the Lean tree that imports it [S33:2] [S33:13]; the exact determinant value produced is $q^{-\theta}$ [S15:188–189]. The bibliographies make the cross-family web explicit at the manuscript level, which the ledger's companion columns do not record: the October 4 manuscript cites the zero-divisor manuscript of family 196 [S6:127] and the characteristic-two manuscript of this family [S6:135]; the characteristic-two manuscript cites the determinant manuscript [S13:219] and the type-$F_\infty$ manuscript of family 250 [S13:227]; the determinant manuscript cites the characteristic-two paper as its input, noting it uses “{{55}}” [S16:40].

The published reasoning trace covers the characteristic-two line. Its Part I prompt asks for the unrestricted positive-characteristic form of the conjecture to be resolved by a complete proof or a rigorous counterexample, and the run delivers a counterexample; its Part II takes Part I's asserted field, group, and sums as its single input and derives the cellular automaton from them, rather than stopping at the statement that the group is nonsurjunctive. The trace ships as a PDF [S36] and is listed for this family in the traces table in `README.md`.

Two caveats for anyone citing this family. The construction is specified, not executed: the characteristic-two manuscript says the prescription “{{56}}” [S9:18] and the odd-characteristic manuscript says it “{{57}}” [S19:28–29]. And the determinant manuscript has a source-level defect: its main file inputs a `glyphtounicode-cmex.tex` file that the tree does not ship, so the LaTeX as published is not self-contained. That is a fact about the published source, recorded here without attempting a build.
