## undergraduate

Take a **flat three-torus** — a cube whose opposite faces are glued
together, like a video game world where walking off the right edge
wraps you to the left. It is a "donut-shaped" 3-dimensional box with
no edges and no corners (in the smooth sense).

The **isoperimetric problem** asks: for a given volume of liquid you
pour into this space, what shape of container (a region) has the
smallest possible surface area? In flat Euclidean space the answer is
always a ball. In a torus, the answer changes as the volume grows:

- For **small volumes**, a ball is best.
- At a **transition** (volume $4\pi/81 \approx 0.155$), the ball is
  tied with a **tube wrapping around the torus** (a cylinder that
  goes through the hole).
- At a **second transition** (volume $1/\pi \approx 0.318$), the
  tube is tied with a **slab** (a flat region between two opposite
  faces).
- For **large volumes**, the complement of the small-volume minimizer
  wins (the "hole" is the minimizer).

This paper **determines the exact transition volumes** and **proves
the classification**: at every volume, the minimizer is exactly one
of these four types (or their complements), and at the two transition
volumes, the two adjacent types tie for the minimum.

## graduate

**Main theorem** [S3:40–46, S2:808, S4:9].
The isoperimetric profile of the unit cubic flat three-torus
$`T^3 = \mathbb R^3 / \mathbb Z^3`$ is determined: at every volume
$`0 < V < 1`$, a finite-perimeter isoperimetric minimizer exists, and
the minimizers, up to null-set changes, are exactly (a) round balls
for $`V \le 4\pi/81`$, (b) round balls or circular tubes about
shortest closed geodesics for $`V = 4\pi/81`$, (c) circular tubes for
$`4\pi/81 < V < 1/\pi`$, (d) tubes or coordinate slabs for
$`V = 1/\pi`$, (e) coordinate slabs for $`1/\pi < V < 1/2`$, and
their complements for $`V > 1/2`$. Every minimizer has the
candidate-profile perimeter, and the classification includes all
equality cases at the two transition volumes.

## history

The **isoperimetric problem on the flat torus** is a classical
question in geometric measure theory. In dimension two, the isoperimetric
profile of the flat 2-torus was determined by Lawlor and Morgan
(1993): the minimizers are balls (small volume), tubes (medium), and
their complements, with a single transition at volume $\pi/2$. The
dimension-three case was open.

The **novel contribution** is the determination of the full
three-dimensional profile with two transition volumes
($4\pi/81$ and $1/\pi$), the classification of all four minimizer
types (balls, tubes, slabs, complements), and the equality-case
analysis at both transitions. The proof draws on the classification
of stable minimal surfaces in the torus (the "tubes" are
cylinders around the shortest closed geodesics of length 1, the
"slabs" are regions between parallel coordinate hyperplanes).

**Prior (my training data, not this repo).** As of my training
cutoff, the isoperimetric profile of the flat 3-torus is a genuine
open problem in geometric measure theory. The 2D case (Lawlor–Morgan)
is classical; the 3D determination with two transitions is the
claimed novelty. I cannot verify the analysis of stable minimal
surfaces in the 3-torus independently from training alone.

## consequences

- **The full isoperimetric profile of the cubic 3-torus is
  determined** [S3:40–46, S2:808, S4:9]: the profile is piecewise
  given by the four candidate families (balls, tubes, slabs,
  complements), with two transition volumes.
- **The transition volumes are explicit** [S3:40–46, S2:808]:
  $`4\pi/81`$ (ball-to-tube) and $`1/\pi`$ (tube-to-slab), with
  exactly the adjacent two types minimizing at each.
- **All minimizers are classified** [S4:9]: up to null-set changes,
  no other shapes achieve the minimum at any volume.
- **The equality cases at the transitions are complete** [S4:9]:
  the classification includes the degenerate cases where two types
  tie.
