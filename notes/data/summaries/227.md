## undergraduate

The **Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) model** is the
mean-field spin glass: $n$ spins interact through a fully
connected random network. The **heat-bath dynamics** is the
most natural Markov chain on the spin space: at each step,
pick a random spin and flip it according to the
Boltzmann weight. The **mixing time** is how long the chain
takes to reach equilibrium from the worst starting point.
This family determines the mixing behavior across the
**entire temperature range**: fast (exponential) at high
temperature, critical (polylogarithmic $n^{2/3}$) at the
transition, and slow (subexponential) below the transition
[S3:58–66 … S10:9–14, S2:539].

## graduate

**High-temperature spectral gap**
[S5:9–13, S2:539]. For every fixed
$0<\beta<1$, the unspectral gap of single-site
heat-bath dynamics is bounded away from zero with
probability tending to one. Equivalently, the Gibbs law
satisfies a dimension-free Poincaré inequality
for all functions.

**High-temperature cutoff**
[S6:10–15, S2:539]. Worst-case total-variation
cutoff for $0\le\beta<1$. The cutoff location is
$\log n/(2\lambda(\beta))$ for rate-one update,
where $\lambda(\beta)>0$ is a deterministic rate.

**Critical mixing exponent**
[S10:9–14, S2:539]. At $\beta=1$, the
worst-start mixing time is $n^{2/3+o(1)}$
in rate-one time, or $n^{5/3+o(1)}$
in attempted uniform-site updates, in probability
over the disorder.

**Critical slowing down**
[S7:94–95, S2:539]. From typical equilibrium
configurations held fixed as initial states, for every
$t_n=o(n^{2/3})$ in rate-one time, the Gibbs mass of
states whose TV distance from equilibrium at time $t_n$
exceeds $1/4$ tends to one.

**Stretched-exponential barriers**
[S8:10–16, S2:539]. At $\beta>1$, from
typical equilibrium states, TV distance at time
$\exp(n^{1/10000})$ exceeds $1/4$ with
Gibbs mass tending to one.

**Subexponential mixing**
[S9:27–33, S2:539]. At $\beta>1$, from a
typical Gibbs-sampled initial state, TV distance is
at most $1/4$ by time
$\exp(n^{1-1/40000000})$, with joint
probability tending to one over the disorder and
the sampled state.

**Critical quench autocorrelations**
[S3:58–66, S2:539]. Joint functional
convergence of the stationary and quench
autocorrelations at $\beta=1$, with the same
random limit for Gaussian and Rademacher couplings.
Mean spin autocorrelations scale as $n^{1/3}$,
time as $n^{2/3}$.

**Functional universality**
[S4:9–15, S2:539]. The stationary spin
autocorrelation at $\beta=1$ has a common
functional scaling limit for Gaussian and
Rademacher couplings. The limit is not a point
mass: it retains sample-to-sample randomness.

## history

The **SK model** (Sherrington–Kirkpatrick, 1975)
is the canonical mean-field spin glass. The **high-temperature
phase** ($\beta<1$) is well-understood: the Gibbs
measure is close to a product measure, and the dynamics
mixes rapidly. The **critical point** ($\beta=1$)
marks the transition to the glassy phase ($\beta>1$),
where the dynamics slows dramatically. The **cutoff
phenomenon** at high temperature
[S6:10–15] establishes that mixing is
sudden: the chain is far from equilibrium just before
the cutoff time and close just after. The **critical
exponent** $n^{2/3}$
[S10:9–14, S7:94–95]
is the mean-field counterpart of the
dynamical exponent in the Edwards–Anderson
theory. The **subexponential** low-temperature bounds
[S8:10–16, S9:27–33]
quantify the gap between the stretched-exponential
lower barrier and the subexponential upper mixing
bound. The **autocorrelation universality**
[S3:58–66, S4:9–15]
establishes that the critical dynamics is
independent of the coupling distribution (Gaussian
vs. Rademacher) at the level of the scaling limit.

**Prior (my training data, not this repo).** The
SK model, the high-temperature phase, and the
cutoff phenomenon for mean-field models are standard.
The precise critical exponent $n^{2/3}$, the
stretched-exponential / subexponential barrier
bounds, and the coupling-distribution universality
of the critical autocorrelations are the novel
contributions. I cannot confirm these independently
from training alone.

## consequences

- **The full-temperature mixing picture**
  [S5:9–13, S6:10–15, S10:9–14]:
  exponential mixing above $\beta=1$, polylogarithmic
  $n^{2/3+o(1)}$ at $\beta=1$, and subexponential
  below.
- **The critical exponent**
  [S10:9–14, S7:94–95]:
  $n^{2/3+o(1)}$ worst-start mixing time, with
  matching slow-mixing lower bounds.
- **The autocorrelation universality**
  [S3:58–66, S4:9–15]: the critical
  scaling limit is the same for Gaussian and
  Rademacher couplings, with non-trivial
  disorder randomness in the limit.
