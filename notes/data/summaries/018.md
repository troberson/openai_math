## undergraduate

Take a group *G* that is absolutely almost simple and simply connected, defined over a global field *k* — the ring of integers of a number field, or the function field of a curve over a finite field. The object of study is *G*(*k*), the group of its rational points, regarded as a plain abstract group: no topology, no finiteness, no generation condition. What the conjecture claims is that this abstract group is nevertheless completely described by finitely many compact local groups. Those local groups are the groups *G*(*k*ᵥ) at the places *v* where *G* is anisotropic, meaning of rank zero over the completion *k*ᵥ; there are only finitely many such places, each local group is compact, and their product *H*\_A carries the product topology. The diagonal embedding δ\_A : *G*(*k*) → *H*\_A then gives the claim: every normal subgroup of *G*(*k*) that is not central is exactly the full preimage under δ\_A of an open normal subgroup of *H*\_A.

The reason this is a theorem and not a definition is that the easy half is much weaker than the hard half. Margulis's normal subgroup theorem already says that a noncentral normal subgroup of an isotropic simple group has finite index, and finite index is a statement about the *existence* of a local topology. It does not say *which* local topology. Approximation arguments give the closure of a subgroup inside *H*\_A, which is a local statement; the theorem wants an equality between an abstract subgroup and the preimage of a closed local subgroup. The gap between the two is a finite group, and killing that finite group is the whole content of the proof.

The family ships the two halves of *every global field* as two manuscripts. The number-field manuscript (September 23, 2026) proves the conjecture over number fields; the function-field manuscript (October 5, 2026) proves it over global function fields, in every positive characteristic, including characteristic two. The function-field manuscript says plainly that it is built on the number-field one, and lists which of its ingredients come from where.

**Proof sketch.** Both manuscripts run on the same skeleton, which is a reduction to a finite group followed by an argument that the finite group is trivial. Start with a noncentral normal subgroup *N*. Finite index gives an exponent *e* that annihilates *G*(*k*)/*N*, so *N* contains the subgroup *R* generated by all *e*th powers. Close *R* inside the anisotropic local product, pull the closure back to *G*(*k*), and form the finite quotient *V* = *V*₀/*R*. Approximation gives the closure; it does not give *R* itself, and *V* is precisely the measured difference between an abstract power subgroup and the subgroup its local closure prescribes. The theorem is *V* = 1 for every *e*.

The structural reductions leave very little to prove. Over a function field, Harder's theorem restricts the anisotropic groups to type *A*, the isotropic case is handled by the global Kneser–Tits theorem, and the inner type-*A* case was settled by Rapinchuk–Segev–Seitz, so what remains is anisotropic outer forms of type *A*: special unitary groups *S* = SU(*D*, \*) attached to a central simple algebra *D* of degree *n* ≥ 3 over a quadratic extension. The proof then proceeds by induction on *n*, simultaneously over all global function fields.

Two arithmetic constructions make the finite group tractable. The first produces a rational point in an actual prescribed coset of *R* — not merely in a congruence class modulo a large power — by multiplying conjugates of torus powers, controlling finitely many places directly and a codimension-two reduction locus at the rest, with scalar invariance recovering the place omitted from strong approximation. The second is a norm torus with both the Hasse principle and weak approximation, which converts locally solvable norm conditions into exact rational solutions. With those in hand, commuting approximation makes the action of the full unitary group *U*(*k*) on *V* preserve conjugacy classes, and a nontrivial *V* yields one of two obstructions: a finite abelian character of *U*(*k*) that is nontrivial on *S*(*k*), or a homomorphism from *U*(*k*) onto a finite nonabelian simple group that is surjective on *V*₀ and kills *R*. The first is ruled out by an algebraic difference function with enough exact invariances; the second by a finite coloring of Hermitian elements, an invariant probability law with uniform color marginals, a finite-group lemma constraining returns between colors, and additive recurrence in characteristic *p* forcing the color set to be translation invariant — which leaves only the empty set and the whole group. Odd division degree is then extended to all degrees by induction through smaller unitary groups and quaternion norm-one groups, with a separate two-norm correction in degree four.

The characteristic-two part is where the function-field proof genuinely departs from the number-field one. The number-field argument gets its invariance from an integer-dilation recurrence supplied by the Furstenberg–Katznelson density theorem; over a function field the additive group has finite exponent, so dilation cannot produce the invariance. The replacement is finite additive Fourier analysis: orthogonality on finite additive subgroups and a quadratic parametrization in odd characteristic, additive polynomials and finite-dimensional coordinates over power subfields in characteristic two.

Prior (my training data, not this repo.) The Margulis–Platonov conjecture was, as of my cutoff, a long-standing open problem in the arithmetic theory of algebraic groups, with the inner type-*A* case settled by Rapinchuk–Segev–Seitz and the remaining anisotropic outer forms of type *A*, together with *D*₄ and *E*₆, open; the function-field case was generally treated as accessible relative to the number-field case because Harder's classification narrows the anisotropic groups. A proof covering both field types, with characteristic two included and with abstract normal subgroups and no finite-generation hypothesis, is the kind of result that closes a problem the field had treated as a named open conjecture for decades. That paragraph is my prior, not a repo claim; the repo's own history is the citations above.
