## contents

The release's blurb [S1:4600] and the catalog abstract [S2:483] state
the family's claim: a countable characteristic-zero division ring that
is algebraic over its center and generated by two elements over that
center, but has infinite dimension over it — a negative answer to
Kurosh's division-ring problem on local finiteness. The manuscript's
abstract [S3:12–15]: "{{1}}"

## undergraduate

A division ring $D$ is algebraic over its center $F=Z(D)$ if every
element satisfies a nonzero polynomial in $F[t]$. It is *locally
finite* if the subring generated over $F$ by any finite set of elements
is finite-dimensional. Kurosh's problem, posed in 1941, asks whether
algebraicity over the center forces local finiteness [S4:4–17,
S4:36–66]. The manuscript answers no, with a concrete two-generated
example [S4:19–26]:

> {{2}}

The pair $\{x,y\}$ generates all of $D$ as an $F$-algebra, $D$ is
infinite-dimensional over $F$, yet every element is algebraic over
$F$ — so the ring is algebraic but not locally finite.

## graduate

**Why the construction is hard.** The known positive results all
impose a uniformity the example lacks. Jacobson's bounded-degree theorem
and Kaplansky's theorem make algebraic algebras satisfying a polynomial
identity locally finite, and Bell–Drensky–Sharifi bound the dimension by
$d^2$ over the center when the relations have uniformly bounded degree
$d$ [S4:36–80]. The construction therefore imposes element-dependent
relations with no uniform degree bound, using an annihilating
polynomial that is allowed to depend on the element. Countability is
essential: it lets the construction schedule every polynomial
expression [S4:36–80].

**The lifting theorem.** The engine is a lifting theorem, Theorem
`thm:lifting`, whose input is a tuple of formal series in two noncommuting
variables over a finite-dimensional central division algebra. It makes
any prescribed series algebraic over a larger countable central field
while increasing the degree of the division algebra, preserving all
previous polynomial identities, and restoring both the two-element
generation and two independent deformation directions — the
requirements that let the iteration continue
[S4:84–139]. The proof combines division-algebra models (symbol and
cyclic algebras), trace-organized cyclic word relations, and inner
derivations for infinitesimal conjugation, realized over the center of a
cyclic division algebra and transported by a Taylor translation
[S4:36–80, S4:84–139].

## history

Kurosh formulated the question in 1941 in connection with Burnside's
finiteness problem for groups, asking whether a division ring algebraic
and finitely generated as an algebra over a central subfield must be
finite-dimensional over it. Golod's finitely generated infinite-
dimensional nil algebras give algebraic unitizations, but they carry
nonzero nilpotents and so do not answer the division-ring version.
Amitsur showed that a division algebra finitely generated as an algebra
over an *uncountable* central field is algebraic — a different
conclusion (algebraicity, not local finiteness), and the countability
here plays the opposite role [S4:36–80]. Goodearl and Letzter address
a related non-finite-generation question for transcendental skew
fields, explicitly distinct from the algebraic Kurosh problem this
manuscript resolves [S4:36–80].

## consequences

**Kurosh's problem, division rings, negative.** The theorem gives a
negative answer to Kurosh's division-ring problem: algebraicity over the
center does not imply local finiteness, and a two-element generating set
is enough to witness the failure [S4:4–17, S4:19–33].

**A two-generated infinite-dimensional algebraic division ring.** The
example is simultaneously countable, characteristic-zero, two-generated
as an algebra over its center, and infinite-dimensional over it — all
four properties at once — which is the sharp form of the negative answer
[S4:19–33].

**Relation to the nil-algebra finiteness questions.** The construction
is a division-ring analogue of the Golod-type negative finiteness
phenomenon, but without nilpotents, so it addresses the division-ring
finiteness questions where the nil constructions do not reach
[S4:36–66].
