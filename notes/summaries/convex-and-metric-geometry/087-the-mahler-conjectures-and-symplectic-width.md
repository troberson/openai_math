# 087 — The Mahler conjectures and symplectic width (Convex and metric geometry, 3 manuscripts, tier T3)

> **087. The Mahler conjectures, functional inequalities and polar-product symplectic width.** Resolves the symmetric and nonsymmetric geometric Mahler conjectures in every dimension, with Hanner polytopes and simplices as the respective volume-product minimizers and all equality cases classified. ## Undergraduate summary

Mahler's question asks how small a convex body and its polar can be at the same time. Take a body *K* — a compact convex set with interior — and form its polar *K*°, the set of directions whose pairing with every point of *K* stays at most 1. Multiply the two volumes. Translation moves this product around, so the interesting quantity is the best one you can achieve by sliding the body: the infimum over translation vectors, attained at a single point called the Santaló point. Mahler asked for the minimum of that quantity, and the answer splits by symmetry. If *K* is symmetric about the origin, the product is never below 4^n/n!, and equality happens exactly for Hanner polytopes and their invertible linear images. If *K* is an arbitrary convex body, the floor is (n+1)^(n+1)/(n!)^2 and the only minimizers are simplices. The cube and the simplex are the two extremal shapes, and the release settles both questions in every dimension [S1:2154] [S5:43–46] [S15:1–5].

All three manuscripts in the release carry the same date, September 22, 2026 [S4:5] [S14:5] [S23:5]. The third manuscript travels a longer road to the symmetric case. For a symmetric body *K*, look at the open set *K* × *K*° in phase space, which has 2n coordinates. Gromov's nonsqueezing theorem says symplectic maps cannot compress a ball past a certain size, so each such open set carries a largest ball size it can absorb — its Gromov width. The release proves that width is exactly 4 for every symmetric *K* in every dimension at least 2, by building an explicit smooth symplectic embedding of a ball of any capacity below 4 [S24:37–43] [S25:23–29]. Volume is preserved by symplectic maps, so the volume of *K* × *K*° is at least the volume of a ball of capacity *c*, and letting *c* approach 4 gives the Mahler lower bound. The proof therefore reaches the conjecture from outside convex geometry, through capacity rather than through sections, projections, or functional inequalities [S25:41–44].

**Proof sketch.** The symmetric proof is built around a planar domain called a lens. A conformal map of the disk, a rotated version of the map Gross uses in the conformal Skorokhod embedding, produces a convex domain whose boundary has a uniformly distributed vertical coordinate [S7:3–5] [S7:11–12] [S7:17–18]. That uniform law converts a complex-analytic feasibility probability into a real volume [S7:23–24]. A holomorphic mass estimate turns the order of a zero into an integral bound for Jacobian minors, so the feasibility probabilities of finitely many strip constraints sum to at least 1 [S9:4–12] [S11:3–6] [S11:40]. That sum is an exact integral over a strip body, and the simplices it counts live inside the polar body, so their missing volume is the quantity the equality argument must drive to zero [S10:3–6] [S12:4–9]. The chain closes with an inequality that is analytic on one side and geometric on the other, and approximation extends it from polytopes to all symmetric bodies [S6:168–174]. Identifying the equality cases uses a metric-median condition: a body that attains the sharp product forces every triple of points to have a metric median, which is the same property as the three-ball intersection condition, and the classical criterion of Lima classifies the finite-dimensional spaces with that property as exactly the spaces built from the line by finitely many ℓ1 and ℓ∞ sums — whose unit balls are Hanner polytopes [S8:157–168] [S8:202–209] [S8:212–213]. The general-body proof runs through cones. The cone–Laplace correspondence of Klartag reduces the inequality to a paired bound on two cone sections [S17:90–91] [S17:100–102]. Moreau's decomposition supplies the paired maps, and change of variables plus Jensen's inequality turn their Jacobians into a lower bound for the normalized logarithm of the Laplace product [S17:105–120]. The hard part is that the projection derivative matrices need not commute, so estimating them one at a time would lose both the constant and the equality case [S17:127–130]. Equality forces them to commute and to be diagonal in one fixed basis, which splits the cone into one-dimensional half-lines, and a bounded section of such a cone is a simplex [S17:143–146].

## Graduate summary

Restated precisely, the release ships three theorems with two constants. Symmetric: for every integer n ≥ 1 and every origin-symmetric convex body *K* ⊂ **R**^n, the volume product is at least 4^n/n!, with equality if and only if *K* is a linear Hanner body [S6:34–41]. General: for every integer n ≥ 1 and every convex body *K* ⊂ **R**^n, the infimum over translation vectors of |K| · |(K − z)°| is at least (n+1)^(n+1)/(n!)^2, with equality if and only if *K* is a simplex [S17:26–32]. Symplectic: for every integer n ≥ 2 and every origin-symmetric convex body *K* ⊂ **R**^n the Gromov width of int *K* × int *K*° is 4, more precisely a smooth symplectic embedding of the standard ball of capacity *c* exists for every *c* strictly below 4 [S25:23–29]. The symplectic statement has no hypotheses at all on *K* beyond symmetry, and it needs no embedding at the capacity-4 endpoint [S25:32–34].

The moves worth naming are these. The symmetric argument is a measure-transport construction, not a geometric inequality in the classical sense: the lens converts a boundary uniform law into volumes [S6:145–146], the Jacobian integrals concentrate on boundary samples and force the analytic half of a two-sided bound [S6:162–169], the geometric half comes from an inclusion, and the same identity controls the missing volume |*A*°| − |Σ_X| that the equality argument consumes [S6:171–174]. Equality is then raised by induction: set *Q* = *K*° and pass to the body in dimension *d* = *n* + 1, where the norm-sum formula preserves equality [S6:176–181]. The functional corollaries are consequences, not inputs. For every even proper lower-semicontinuous convex potential *φ*, the product of the Laplace transform of *φ* and that of its Legendre transform is at least 4^n [S6:211–216], and the sharp constant is attained by φ(x) = Σ|x_i|, whose transform is the indicator of [−1, 1]^n with both integrals equal to 2^n [S6:245–248]. On the general side the functional bound is e^n, obtained through the implication of Fradelizi and Meyer, which uses the geometric theorem in every dimension at once and requires no centering or normalization of *φ* [S18:23–25] [S18:32–34] [S17:183–187]. The same functional bound feeds an entropy–transport inequality with additive constant −3n, and the constant is explained by Gozlan's equivalence, applied with *c* = e [S18:78] [S18:83–87]. The general manuscript's own assembly records where the heavy lifting sits: its consequences section is placed ahead of the appendix, and the scalar and segment arguments are appendices [S16:43–54], at 1,012 and 1,196 lines.

What the release does not say is the most load-bearing part. The functional Mahler inequalities are stated in the manuscripts and in the release blurb, and they are not part of the formalized statement: the scope note excludes them explicitly [S29:13] [S29:17]. The symplectic theorem makes no claim about equality cases [S25:41–44], imposes no smoothness, strict convexity, unconditional symmetry, or product structure [S25:32–34], and asserts no embedding at capacity exactly 4 [S29:21]. The general proof's numerical layer is a finite-arithmetic certificate, not sampling: "Numerical sampling is not used as a substitute for these arguments" [S17:153–154], and the verification bundle states its own scope — "These commands check the specified finite arithmetic. The manuscript supplies the analytic and geometric arguments that use it" [S20:47–48]. The symplectic paper uses neither the volume-product theorem nor the equality classification of its companion [S26:66–67], so the symmetric result is proved twice by independent routes, once by the lens and once by ball embeddings.

## History of the conjecture

The manuscripts give the history, and it is unusually well documented because the two constants have separate lineages.

Symmetric side. Mahler introduced the volume-product problem through convex bodies and transference in the geometry of numbers [S6:48–50]. Reisner characterized the planar equality cases as parallelograms as a consequence of his zonoid theorem [S6:51–52]. Iriyeh and Shibata proved the complete three-dimensional symmetric theorem including equality [S6:53–54], and Fradelizi, Hubard, Meyer, Roldán-Pensado, and Zvavitch gave a shorter proof based on equipartitions with another equality argument and stability [S6:55–57]. The extremal class comes from Hanner's recursively constructed spaces [S6:62]. Saint-Raymond proved the sharp inequality for unconditional bodies [S6:64–65]; Reisner proved it for zonoids and showed equality in that class means a parallelotope [S6:67–70]. Strict local minimality was established by Nazarov, Petrov, Ryabogin, and Zvavitch for the cube, up to linear equivalence [S6:72–74], and Kim extended it to all Hanner polytopes [S6:75]. The analytic lineage runs through the reverse Blaschke–Santaló inequality: Bourgain and Milman established it [S6:81–82], and Nazarov proved it using Hörmander's ∂̄ theorem and Bergman kernels [S6:85–87]. Karasev proved the sharp inequality for hyperplane sections and projections of ℓp balls [S6:89–90]. The manuscript is explicit that these precedents are not hypotheses of its proof [S6:92–93]. Its bibliography cites the general-body companion in the same release [S13:311–317].

General side. Mahler's polygon paper established the general planar inequality and its triangle equality case among polygons [S17:45–46], and his transference paper formulated the higher-dimensional symmetric problem [S17:47–48]. Meyer gave a new proof of the planar general inequality and completed its equality characterization for arbitrary planar convex bodies, with triangles exactly the minimizers [S17:49–51]. The affine center used in the proof belongs to the classical Santaló theory [S17:51–52]. The dimensional floor under all of this is the inverse Santaló theorem of Bourgain and Milman, which gives an exponential lower bound with an absolute constant [S17:56–59], and the manuscript is explicit that the approaches establishing exponential-order bounds do not give the sharp constant it asserts [S17:66–67]. Progress on the sharp constant was by body class: Meyer and Reisner used shadow systems to prove the sharp inequality and simplex equality for polytopes with at most n + 3 vertices [S17:70–72]; Kim and Reisner established strict local minimality with a quantitative estimate at every simplex [S17:75–77]. The nearest prior dimensional result is the 2026 preprint of Chen, Li, Xi, and Xu, who prove the sharp bound in dimension three with equality exactly for tetrahedra [S17:79–82]. Its bibliography cites the symmetric companion in the same release [S19:292–298].

Symplectic side. Bourgain and Milman's asymptotic reverse Santaló inequality gives the exponential starting point [S26:9–12]. Gromov's nonsqueezing theorem established that ball embeddings detect restrictions beyond volume preservation [S26:17–18], and Viterbo proposed a sharp volume–capacity inequality for convex domains [S26:19–20] and connected it to the symmetric Mahler conjecture [S26:21–22]. The capacity normalization is the Hofer–Zehnder capacity, with value πR² on a ball of radius R [S26:28–29], and the manuscript notes that the matching lower bound requires actual ball embeddings and does not follow from a capacity computation [S26:33–34]. Known cases were partial: Karasev for ℓp unit balls with 1 < p < ∞ [S26:37], and Ramos and Sepe, who identify the interior of the cube–crosspolytope product with the open ball of capacity 4 [S26:40–41]. The manuscript states that it answers positively the unrestricted symmetric-polar-product width question posed in a 2026 reference [S26:47–49].

Two boundary conditions in that history matter for reading the result. The unrestricted Viterbo conjecture was answered negatively by a four-dimensional counterexample built from a regular pentagon and a rotated partner [S26:52–54], and the manuscript records why that does not touch its theorem: "the pentagon is not centrally symmetric" [S26:54–55], and its theorem concerns the particular Lagrangian product of a symmetric body and its polar, making no assertion for arbitrary convex domains in phase space [S26:55–57]. The planar coordinate in the construction is Gross's uniform-distribution map from the conformal Skorokhod construction, rotated in source and target [S26:59–60].

## Consequences now that the proof has been found

The symmetric case is settled, so the open question moves to the sharpness of the functional constants. The geometric theorem implies the functional one, and the implication is one-directional: the limiting implication does not transfer equality cases, so the geometric classification of minimizers does not classify the even potentials for which equality holds [S6:249–251] [S18:46–49]. The two constants are now ordered: 4^n for even potentials, from the symmetric companion, and e^n for general log-concave functions [S18:50–51] [S6:253–255]. The entropy–transport consequence carries additive constant −3n through Gozlan's equivalence [S18:78] [S18:83–87], and the symmetric functional form carries the corresponding constant −n log(4e²) [S6:280].

The symplectic result closes the symmetric case of the volume–capacity program. It resolves the symmetric Mahler conjecture by a route that uses no convex-geometric input [S25:41–44] [S26:66–67], and it supplies the lower bound that a capacity computation alone cannot give [S26:33–34]. Its packing corollary transports finite ball packings into *U_K* whenever the packings satisfy strict volume and pairwise-capacity inequalities [S25:46–48] [S27:227–238], with pairwise disjoint images and a condition that is vacuous for a single ball [S27:236–237]. Sharpness of the constant is demonstrated by the Hanner bodies, whose volume product is 4^n/n! [S27:222–224]. The corollary composes with the release's own ball-packing theorem [S27:243] [S28:224–230], and the symplectic manuscript cites the release's higher-dimensional ball-packing family as family 343 in its bibliography [S28:71–77], so the two published results in this release compose inside a single corollary.

The general theorem settles the nonsymmetric case in every dimension, which closes the line of work that proceeded by body class and by dimension [S17:70–82]. The manuscript states plainly that its numerical layer is a certificate and not evidence by sampling [S17:153–154]. Its verification bundle is a reproducible finite-arithmetic check: Python 3 with SymPy 1.14.0 pinned, run with assertions enabled [S20:3–4] [S14:21], ten programs copied into a fresh directory and run in dependency order after their hashes are checked [S20:15–17], with success requiring exit status 0 and a passed flag in the run record [S20:43–44] [S14:32]. The pinned dependency file states what it is for: "Exact polynomial algebra and formal differentiation." [S22:1] The manifest whose hashes are checked is read by the driver [S21:72] and recorded in its output [S21:86], and it is named in the bundle's own instructions [S20:15–17]; the file itself is not shipped in the release, which is a source-level fact about the bundle as published. The bundle was not executed for this page.

Two gaps follow from the formalization record rather than from the mathematics. The functional inequalities are stated, sharp, and used [S6:211–216] [S17:172–177], and no 087 tree formalizes them — the scope note says so [S29:13] [S29:17], and the general theorem's formal statement is the geometric infimum alone [S29:15–16]. And the general theorem's formal proof exists with no `sorry` [S41:17–33] while the release's own index records neither a bibliography entry for its manuscript [S30:757–760] nor a main result for its challenge [S36]. The published mathematics and the release's index of it are out of step there, in the direction of the index being behind.
