# 006 — Goldfeld's conjecture (Number theory, 2 manuscripts, tier T0)

## Original CONTENTS.md summary

> 006. Goldfeld's conjecture: densities and mean analytic rank. Proves Goldfeld's conjecture for quadratic twists of every elliptic curve over ℚ: analytic ranks zero and one each have density 1/2, and the mean analytic rank tends to 1/2. Both statements order signed squarefree twist parameters by absolute value.

[S1:150] (the analytic-density manuscript entry):

> We prove Goldfeld's analytic density conjecture: for every elliptic curve *E* over ℚ, the quadratic twists of *E* with analytic rank zero and one each have density 1/2 among signed squarefree twist parameters ordered by absolute value. > For every elliptic curve over ℚ, we prove that the average analytic rank of its quadratic twists tends to 1/2 when signed squarefree twist parameters are ordered by absolute value. This resolves Goldfeld's mean analytic-rank conjecture in this counting convention.

## Undergraduate summary

Fix one elliptic curve *E* over the rationals. Its quadratic twists form a huge, explicitly describable family: for each nonzero squarefree integer *d*, twist the curve by *d*. The family is ordered by the absolute value of *d*, and both signs of *d* are counted, so the counting set is the signed squarefree integers of height at most *X*. Each twist has an analytic rank: the order in which its *L*-function vanishes at the centre of the critical strip. The functional equation forces that order to be even for one sign of the twist parameter and odd for the other, so the only possible ranks are 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on, split by sign.

Goldfeld's conjecture, in the form the release uses, says what fraction of this family has each rank: rank zero should occur for half the parameters and rank one for half, and the average rank over the family should therefore be 1/2. The two manuscripts split the problem at its natural seam. The first proves the density statement, and does it by proving something much stronger pointwise: for every curve whose full 2-power Selmer corank is 0 or 1, analytic rank, Mordell–Weil rank and Selmer corank are all equal, and the Tate–Shafarevich group is finite. The second proves that the rare high-rank twists cannot inflate the average: their total contribution to the rank-weighted sum is *o*(*X*).

Read together, the two give the frequency statement and the mean statement for every rational elliptic curve, with no hypothesis on the curve. The density-one set of twists has analytic rank equal to Mordell–Weil rank and a finite Tate–Shafarevich group, which is the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conclusion for half the family in each sign.

**Proof sketch.** The density theorem is a pointwise theorem plus a counting theorem. Smith proved that, for every rational elliptic curve, the full 2-power Selmer corank of the twists is 0 for a density-one half and 1 for the other half. What was missing was the analytic direction: Selmer information does not obviously force the *L*-function not to vanish. The first manuscript supplies exactly that implication at the prime 2. It builds a finite binary family of auxiliary twists, indexed by vertices of 𝔽₂ᵇ, all sharing the same local twisting classes at the bad places. At every nonzero vertex the analytic order is 0 or 1, matching the corank of the fixed curve, and a scalar determinant coordinate attached to the relevant Selmer complex has a nonzero central specialization there with bounded 2-adic valuation. Interpolation then recovers nonvanishing at the missing vertex, which is the original curve. At corank 0 the interpolated class is a Beilinson–Kato class detecting *L*(*E*,1); at corank 1 it is a Heegner class over an imaginary quadratic field whose height detects the derivative of the product of two *L*-functions. The two residual cases are handled by different machinery: reducible *E*[2] is encoded by graphs of prescribed quadratic residue symbols, irreducible *E*[2] by symmetric matrices over 𝔽₂ whose nullities bound the Selmer dimension. The counting input then converts the pointwise converse into densities, and a short density lemma passes from integer parameters to squarefree ones.

The mean theorem needs a different kind of control. Knowing that ranks 0 and 1 each have density 1/2 says nothing about the rank-weighted average, because a density-zero set of twists of very large rank can carry all the mass. The second manuscript proves a tail estimate: for each curve there are constants *C* and *R* such that the ranks above *R* contribute at most *C*/*R* per unit of height, in the limsup. Its argument runs on completed-derivative identities, exponentially smoothed Dirichlet weights, short mollifiers, and a random-model comparison that replaces odd-prime character values by independent variables; a Jensen bound gives the pointwise estimate that ranks grow only logarithmically, and a count of twists with rank above *k*, uniform up to *k* = (log *X*)^(3/5), finishes the tail. Combining the tail with the density input gives mean analytic rank 1/2.

## Graduate summary

The family ships two manuscripts, and each states its own headline theorem. In the analytic-density manuscript, the pointwise result is Theorem 1, the 2-converse [S5:14–19]: for every elliptic curve *E*/ℚ with 2-power Selmer corank *c*₂(*E*) in {0,1}, one has *a*(*E*) = *r*(*E*) = *c*₂(*E*) and Sha(*E*/ℚ) finite. Its scope paragraph is explicit about what is not assumed — “There is no restriction on reduction type, complex multiplication, rational torsion, or rational isogenies” [S5:21–22] — and about what the hypothesis really is: “a Mordell--Weil rank bound alone does not give it.” [S5:23] The density theorem is Theorem 2 [S5:35–46], and its two consequences are stated inside the theorem environment: “Consequently the twists of analytic rank at least two have density zero.” [S5:42] and “For a density-one set of signed squarefree parameters $d$, the analytic and Mordell--Weil ranks of $E^{(d)}$ agree and its whole Tate--Shafarevich group is finite.” [S5:43–45] The paper is candid about the division of labour: “The main work of this paper is the pointwise converse.” [S5:52]

The deduction is short and is given in the conclusions section. Smith's unrestricted theorem gives the corank-zero and corank-one counts as *X*/2 + *o*(*X*) [S6:67–72]; a density lemma turns an infinite set of integers into a density-one subset of squarefree integers [S6:28–29], proved by the Möbius sum ∑*μ*(*m*)/*m*² = 1/*ζ*(2) [S6:44–64]; and the two counts are then identified: “The analytic-rank counts therefore equal the corresponding corank counts exactly.” [S6:80–81] The complement, “the twists of analytic rank at least two, has density zero” [S6:81–82], and the rank and finiteness assertions on the density-one union come from the pointwise converse and the density-one corank count [S6:77–79]. A corollary worth knowing separately is the finite-descent criterion [S5:61–67]: if the 2-descent quantity *d*₂(*E*) is 0 or 1, then *a* = *r* = *c*₂ = *d*₂, Sha is finite, and Sha[*2*^∞] = 0, so a finite 2-descent certifies the analytic rank and kills the whole 2-primary Tate–Shafarevich group. The proof passes from *d*₂ to the full corank through the alternating Cassels–Tate pairing [S5:71–73].

The mean manuscript's headline is Theorem 1 [S10:22–28]: the average analytic rank over the signed squarefree family tends to 1/2. Its input from the companion is a single equation — the density statement for ranks 0 and 1 — and the paper says so: “A zero-density exceptional family can still contribute to a rank-weighted average.” [S10:45–46] The new assertion is the tail estimate, Theorem 2 [S10:50–58], whose proof is independent of the density input. The scope sentence is the same shape as the companion's: “It applies without restrictions on complex multiplication, rational torsion, rational isogenies, or reduction type.” [S10:31–33] The algebraic-rank moment corollary [S10:79–91] gives exp(*t*·*r*) mean (1+e^*t*)/2 and *r*^*m* mean 1/2, and its non-claims are listed: “The corollary concerns algebraic ranks only: it gives no higher analytic-rank moments, no uniformity for parameters varying with \(Y\), and no moment limit for thin polynomial subfamilies.” [S10:97–99] The analytic assumptions are stated once and clearly: “The proof invokes neither the Birch--Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture nor the generalized Riemann hypothesis.” [S10:165–166] Modularity supplies the continuation and functional equation for every curve in the argument [S10:162–163].

What the family does not say is as important as what it says. It does not prove the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer leading-coefficient formula: the density manuscript states that “The rank and finiteness assertions are distinct from the Birch--Swinnerton-Dyer leading-coefficient formula.” [S5:90–91] — the leading-coefficient part is family 002's, and the two combine (see [002 — The full Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer formula in Selmer coranks zero and one](002-full-birch-swinnerton-dyer-formula-from-low-selmer-corank.md)). It proves no analytic-rank moments beyond the mean. It does not claim the mean over thin polynomial subfamilies, nor uniformity in parameters that grow with the height. It does not claim the density statement in Goldfeld's original discriminant ordering; both manuscripts count signed squarefree integers, and the mean manuscript flags the difference explicitly [S10:106–110]. It uses no claim from the Hanners preprint, and says why: “Hanners claims the full Birch--Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture for every rational elliptic curve” [S10:155–156], “This is not a refutation of the claimed theorem.” [S10:160] The preprint is a bibliography entry of the mean manuscript [S11:17–25].

## History of the conjecture

Goldfeld's 1979 note “Conjectures on elliptic curves over quadratic fields” [S7:3] is the source, cited by both manuscripts at the same place: page 113, Conjecture (B) [S5:110]. Its formulation is about the mean analytic order in a family parametrized by quadratic-field discriminants [S10:106–108]. The release's counting convention is not that one: both manuscripts count signed squarefree integers ordered by absolute value, and the mean manuscript states the substitution plainly [S10:106–110]. Any statement of what was proved should keep that convention attached, since the density and mean claims are convention-dependent.

The route the proof takes runs through Selmer statistics. Smith's progression is recorded in the density manuscript: higher 2-power Selmer distributions for curves with full rational 2-torsion and no rational cyclic subgroup of order four; then the irreducible residual case and further rational 2-isogeny cases; then the unrestricted theorem, which gives full Selmer coranks 0 and 1 with density 1/2 each for every rational elliptic curve [S5:150–157]. That last theorem is cited in the bibliography as “The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture implies” [S7:18], and the manuscript is precise about what it borrows and what it supplies: Smith's analytic corollary assumes Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer [S5:158–159], while “We use the Selmer theorem itself and supply the pointwise analytic implication” [S5:160–161]

The converse problem itself has a long history, and the density manuscript situates it. Gross–Zagier and Kolyvagin gave the forward implication: analytic rank at most 1 forces equality with the Mordell–Weil rank and finiteness of Sha [S5:165–169]. The converse was known in restricted settings: Skinner–Urban at good ordinary odd primes under residual irreducibility and ramification hypotheses; Skinner and Zhang for rank one through Heegner points; Burungale–Castella–Skinner removing the auxiliary ramification conditions for *p* > 3; Castella–Wan at good supersingular primes *p* > 3 for semistable curves; Castella–Grossi–Lee–Skinner and Keller–Yin for reducible residual representations at Eisenstein primes; and a substantial CM literature at the prime 2, ending with Burungale–Tian's corank-zero theorem covering every rational CM curve at every prime with no reduction restriction [S5:171–232]. The dyadic case is where this family sits, and it is the case the prior literature handled with hypotheses.

Prior (my training data, not this repo.) The 2-power Selmer side of this story is dominated by Smith's 2024 Annals paper on the 2^∞-Selmer group of quadratic twists, and the analytic side by the Iwaniec–Luo–Sarnak and Perelli–Pomykała line of moment results; the mean-rank conjecture was generally regarded as out of reach unconditionally, with the strongest results conditional on GRH. A proof of the density statement for every rational curve, at the prime 2, with no restrictions, is the kind of result that would have been listed as a major open problem a decade earlier. That paragraph is my prior, not a repo claim; the repo's own history is the two citations above.

## Consequences now that the proof has been found

The family's own bibliography records the intra-corpus edges, and they point at family 002 in both directions. The density manuscript cites the mean manuscript as the companion that supplies the tail estimate and the mean [S7:1054–1060], cites the all-prime Selmer converse [S7:899–905] and says of it: “Its proof uses the $2$-converse and analytic twist densities established here.” [S5:87–88] That companion is family 002's Selmer-converse manuscript. It cites the two-primary BSD manuscript [S7:891–897] and derives a joint consequence: combined with the Selmer-corank densities, the two-primary formula holds on a density-one set of signed squarefree twists [S5:92–99]. Family 002's own entry states the same pairing from the other side: “With result 006, this gives full BSD for a density-one set of quadratic twists of every elliptic curve over ℚ.” [S1:32] So the chain 002 ↔ 006 is recorded by the release in both directions, and the triage ledger lists it as one of the three cross-family chains inside T0: “Cross-family chains inside T0: 001↔032, 002↔006, and 311⇒Hovey–Strickland.” [S14:854] “A chain is only as strong as its weakest, unformalized link.” [S14:854–855]

Concretely, the joint consequence is the strongest single statement the pair produces: for every elliptic curve over ℚ, for a density-one set of signed squarefree twist parameters, the analytic and Mordell–Weil ranks are equal, the whole Tate–Shafarevich group is finite, and the full BSD leading-term formula holds. The rank part and the Sha part come from 006; the leading-coefficient part comes from 002.

Two further consequences are inside the manuscripts. The finite-descent criterion turns a finite 2-descent computation into a certified analytic rank and a vanishing 2-primary Sha, in the two cases *d*₂ ∈ {0,1} [S5:61–67] — a practical statement, since a 2-descent is computable. And the algebraic-rank moment corollary gives the full moment sequence of the Mordell–Weil rank in the signed squarefree family, mean 1/2 for every fixed power [S10:79–91], which is a statement about rational points rather than about *L*-functions.

What is not a consequence: BSD for a fixed curve, BSD for a positive proportion in Goldfeld's discriminant ordering, the leading coefficient for curves of corank ≥ 2, or any statement about the twists of analytic rank ≥ 2 beyond their density being zero.
