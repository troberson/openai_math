## Undergraduate summary

Take a group *G* that is absolutely almost simple and simply connected, defined over a global field *k* — the ring of integers of a number field, or the function field of a curve over a finite field. The object of study is *G*(*k*), the group of its rational points, regarded as a plain abstract group: no topology, no finiteness, no generation condition. What the conjecture claims is that this abstract group is nevertheless completely described by finitely many compact local groups. Those local groups are the groups *G*(*k*ᵥ) at the places *v* where *G* is anisotropic, meaning of rank zero over the completion *k*ᵥ; there are only finitely many such places, each local group is compact, and their product *H*\_A carries the product topology. The diagonal embedding δ\_A : *G*(*k*) → *H*\_A then gives the claim: every normal subgroup of *G*(*k*) that is not central is exactly the full preimage under δ\_A of an open normal subgroup of *H*\_A.

The reason this is a theorem and not a definition is that the easy half is much weaker than the hard half. Margulis's normal subgroup theorem already says that a noncentral normal subgroup of an isotropic simple group has finite index, and finite index is a statement about the *existence* of a local topology. It does not say *which* local topology. Approximation arguments give the closure of a subgroup inside *H*\_A, which is a local statement; the theorem wants an equality between an abstract subgroup and the preimage of a closed local subgroup. The gap between the two is a finite group, and killing that finite group is the whole content of the proof.

The family ships the two halves of *every global field* as two manuscripts. The number-field manuscript (September 23, 2026) proves the conjecture over number fields; the function-field manuscript (October 5, 2026) proves it over global function fields, in every positive characteristic, including characteristic two. The function-field manuscript says plainly that it is built on the number-field one, and lists which of its ingredients come from where.

**Proof sketch.** Both manuscripts run on the same skeleton, which is a reduction to a finite group followed by an argument that the finite group is trivial. Start with a noncentral normal subgroup *N*. Finite index gives an exponent *e* that annihilates *G*(*k*)/*N*, so *N* contains the subgroup *R* generated by all *e*th powers. Close *R* inside the anisotropic local product, pull the closure back to *G*(*k*), and form the finite quotient *V* = *V*₀/*R*. Approximation gives the closure; it does not give *R* itself, and *V* is precisely the measured difference between an abstract power subgroup and the subgroup its local closure prescribes. The theorem is *V* = 1 for every *e*.

The structural reductions leave very little to prove. Over a function field, Harder's theorem restricts the anisotropic groups to type *A*, the isotropic case is handled by the global Kneser–Tits theorem, and the inner type-*A* case was settled by Rapinchuk–Segev–Seitz, so what remains is anisotropic outer forms of type *A*: special unitary groups *S* = SU(*D*, \*) attached to a central simple algebra *D* of degree *n* ≥ 3 over a quadratic extension. The proof then proceeds by induction on *n*, simultaneously over all global function fields.

Two arithmetic constructions make the finite group tractable. The first produces a rational point in an actual prescribed coset of *R* — not merely in a congruence class modulo a large power — by multiplying conjugates of torus powers, controlling finitely many places directly and a codimension-two reduction locus at the rest, with scalar invariance recovering the place omitted from strong approximation. The second is a norm torus with both the Hasse principle and weak approximation, which converts locally solvable norm conditions into exact rational solutions. With those in hand, commuting approximation makes the action of the full unitary group *U*(*k*) on *V* preserve conjugacy classes, and a nontrivial *V* yields one of two obstructions: a finite abelian character of *U*(*k*) that is nontrivial on *S*(*k*), or a homomorphism from *U*(*k*) onto a finite nonabelian simple group that is surjective on *V*₀ and kills *R*. The first is ruled out by an algebraic difference function with enough exact invariances; the second by a finite coloring of Hermitian elements, an invariant probability law with uniform color marginals, a finite-group lemma constraining returns between colors, and additive recurrence in characteristic *p* forcing the color set to be translation invariant — which leaves only the empty set and the whole group. Odd division degree is then extended to all degrees by induction through smaller unitary groups and quaternion norm-one groups, with a separate two-norm correction in degree four.

The characteristic-two part is where the function-field proof genuinely departs from the number-field one. The number-field argument gets its invariance from an integer-dilation recurrence supplied by the Furstenberg–Katznelson density theorem; over a function field the additive group has finite exponent, so dilation cannot produce the invariance. The replacement is finite additive Fourier analysis: orthogonality on finite additive subgroups and a quadratic parametrization in odd characteristic, additive polynomials and finite-dimensional coordinates over power subfields in characteristic two.

## Graduate summary

Each manuscript states its own headline theorem, and the two statements are the same shape over the two kinds of field. In the function-field manuscript the theorem is [S5:18–27]: “Let $k$ be a global function field and let $G$ be an absolutely almost simple simply connected $k$-group. If $N\triangleleft G(k)$ and $N\not\subseteq Z(G(k))$, then” *N* = δ\_A⁻¹(*W*) for an open normal subgroup *W* ◁ *H*\_A, and the theorem environment itself records the scope: “This holds in every positive characteristic, including when $G$ is anisotropic over $k$.” [S5:18–27] The number-field theorem is [S11:21–29]: “If $N\triangleleft G(k)$ is not contained in $Z(G(k))$, then there is an open normal subgroup $W\triangleleft H_A$ such that” *N* = δ\_A⁻¹(*W*).

The empty-product case is stated in both, and it is the sharpest part of each claim. In the function-field manuscript: “If $A(G)$ is empty, the product $H_A$ is trivial, and the theorem says that $G(k)$ has no proper noncentral normal subgroup.” [S5:29–31] In the number-field manuscript: “Thus the result resolves the Margulis--Platonov conjecture positively over number fields, including globally anisotropic groups. In the case $A=\varnothing$, the conclusion is $N=G(k)$, not merely that $N$ has finite index or is dense in a local topology.” [S11:31–34] The number-field manuscript is also explicit that its normal subgroups are abstract in the strong sense: “Normal subgroups in this assertion are abstract: no topology or finite-generation condition is imposed on them. We prove the conjecture in this form.” [S11:15–19]

Both manuscripts name the real difficulty, and they name it identically. The function-field version is “finite index alone does not identify which local topology detects a normal subgroup.” [S5:34–36] The number-field version puts it as the finite obstruction: “It remains to show that the finite group $V_0/R$ is trivial. This is the distinction between the desired abstract equality and the closure statement furnished by approximation.” [S11:143–147] The number-field manuscript adds the set-versus-subgroup caveat that the construction needs: “Here $G(k)^e$ is the subgroup generated by powers, not just the set of powers.” [S11:143–147] The function-field manuscript states the same finite group as a measurement: “The group $V$ is finite; it measures the possible difference between an abstract power subgroup and the subgroup prescribed by its local closure.” [S5:159–162] and its target is “We prove $V=1$ for every $e$ by induction on $n$, simultaneously over all global function fields.” [S5:159–162]

The reduction that defines the remaining case is recorded in the function-field introduction. “Over a global function field, Harder's results restrict the anisotropic absolutely almost simple simply connected groups to type~$A$” [S5:78–80]; “The isotropic case follows from the global Kneser--Tits theorem and projective simplicity” [S5:81–82]; and “these results reduce the present problem to anisotropic outer forms of type~$A$, represented by special unitary groups.” [S5:83–85] The manuscript states what that closes: “provides the required normal-subgroup description for these groups in every characteristic.” [S5:89–90] The number-field manuscript describes its own residue the same way — the established cases “include isotropic groups and all inner forms of type $A$, as well as the other types outside the remaining anisotropic outer $A$, trialitarian $D_4$, and $E_6$ cases” [S11:72–74], and it uses that reduction “and supply the remaining arguments here.” [S11:75–76]

The finite-group machinery is where the two proofs are closest, and the function-field manuscript itemizes the borrowing. “We use its approach through finite quotients of abstract power subgroups, approximation within a prescribed coset, and the distinction between finite characters and nonabelian simple quotients.” [S5:94–97] It then names the sources of each piece — the power-factor construction, the finite lattice calculation for the symmetric norm torus, the difference-function and exact-transitivity argument, the commuting approximation and circle-extension reduction, the invariant probability law, the finite-group obstruction, the unitary induction — and states what it adds: “We reproduce the lattice calculation and the finite-group argument. We also verify an additional subgroup-complement property of the chosen finite-group subsets, which is needed for the present recurrence proof.” [S5:109–111] The pieces that do not come from the number-field proof are listed separately: “Several independent inputs retain their own roles. We use Prasad's strong approximation theorem over function fields” [S5:113–116], the cohomological reductions of Bruhat–Tits and Harder [S5:114–116], Demarche–Harari duality for tori and homogeneous spaces, Prasad–Rapinchuk's metaplectic-kernel computation for the circle extension, and Howe–Moore decay for the probability law; the step from a simple quotient to an action of the full unitary group “uses Feit--Seitz's theorem that class-preserving automorphisms of a finite simple group are inner” [S5:120–122].

The characteristic-two change is stated as a specific failure, not as a general difficulty. “The additive group of the field has finite exponent, so the integer-dilation recurrence used in the number-field proof does not give the required invariance.” [S5:133–136] The replacement is: “Odd characteristic uses orthogonality on finite additive subgroups and a quadratic parametrization; characteristic two uses additive polynomials and finite-dimensional coordinates over power subfields.” [S5:139–141] The two constructions that carry the induction are the coset sieve — “products of conjugated torus powers let us choose a rational point in an actual prescribed coset of $R$” [S5:164–175] — and the norm torus: “Second, a particular norm torus has both the Hasse principle and weak approximation. This turns locally solvable simultaneous norm conditions into exact rational solutions.” [S5:171–174] The dichotomy they produce is stated in full: “Commuting approximation makes the action of $U(k)$ on $V$ preserve conjugacy classes. If $V\ne1$, a simple quotient and a circle-extension argument then produce one of two obstructions: a finite abelian character of $U(k)$ nontrivial on $S(k)$, or a homomorphism from $U(k)$ onto a finite nonabelian simple group whose restriction to $V_0$ is surjective and which kills $R$.” [S5:177–183] The terminal step of the palette argument is “Additive recurrence in characteristic $p$ forces the indicator of membership in this subset to be translation invariant.” [S5:190–192] The degree extension is closed by “Degree four requires a separate two-norm correction. Uniform control of local power subgroups allows this induction even when $p$ divides $e$.” [S5:208–210], after which the conclusion section converts *V* = 1 into the theorem [S5:210–211].

The number-field manuscript's additions beyond the shared skeleton are the exceptional types and the uniform-marginal law. “The construction of the uniform-marginal law is an important part of the proof: additive averaging by itself need not preserve all colors.” [S11:195–196] Its outer-type-*A* induction runs “The ternary split-algebra case uses quadratic norm equations on a Ch\^atelet surface.” [S11:212–213] and “Even degree is reduced by a quaternion factorization and a smaller unitary centralizer, with a separate degree-four determinant correction.” [S11:213–215]; *D*₄ is treated by finding “in each relevant rational coset, a regular element whose torus admits a rational inverter” [S11:219–221], and *E*₆ is reduced to a simply connected *D*₄ subgroup and the cover of a root-involution centralizer of type *A*₁*A*₅. Its foundations section is where the shared tools are proved, under names that match the introduction's list: “Known cases of (MP)” [S12:65], “Avoidance along a line” [S12:275], “Power-factor approximation” [S12:405], “Cyclic detection” [S12:615], “A central sign element” [S12:667] and “Hasse principle and approximation for a norm torus” [S12:720].

The conclusion of the function-field proof is short and is worth reading as the shape of the final deduction. “The global Kneser--Tits theorem handles the isotropic case, and the known inner-type-$A$ theorem handles anisotropic inner forms.” [S6:4–7] Finite index gives “the group $S(k)/N$ is finite.” [S6:9–12], the induction gives *R* = *V*₀ = δ\_A⁻¹(*P*₀), so “Thus $N$ is a union of cosets of the open subgroup $\delta_A^{-1}(P_0)$.” [S6:17–18], and taking *W* to be the inverse image of the corresponding subgroup of *H*\_A/*P*₀ gives “Then $W$ is open and normal, and $N=\delta_A^{-1}(W)$. Density also shows that $W=\overline{\delta_A(N)}$, so this local subgroup is uniquely determined by $N$.” [S6:24–27] The empty case closes the statement: “If $A$ is empty, $H_A$ is trivial and the same argument gives $N=S(k)$.” [S6:27–28]

What the family does not say. It does not claim the congruence subgroup problem. The number-field manuscript has a CSP corollary, and it is scoped tightly: the corollary concludes “Then the $S$-congruence kernel is trivial:” *C*^*S*(*G*) = {1} under a positive Dirichlet-density hypothesis [S11:95–117], its equivalence with the classical congruence subgroup property being “every finite-index normal subgroup contains a principal congruence subgroup of some nonzero ideal level” [S11:95–117], and its proof is an application of Rapinchuk's positive-density theorem with the conjecture as a hypothesis — the manuscript's own theorem “is the Margulis--Platonov statement assumed in” that theorem [S11:120–121]. The non-claims are listed: “The corollary is restricted to the stated positive-density range: it does not address Serre's finite-$S$ conjecture, arbitrary infinite $S$, or centrality or finiteness of congruence kernels outside that range.” [S11:128–131] The manuscript also refuses to use the conjecture circularly: “That relationship does not justify using a theorem which assumes the conjecture to prove it.” [S11:82–83] Neither manuscript claims anything about the congruence completion itself, and the function-field manuscript states no CSP consequence at all.

## History of the conjecture

Both manuscripts give the same origin story, in the same terms. The function-field manuscript: “Its origins lie in Kneser's simplicity question for quaternion norm-one groups and Platonov's extension of that question to simply connected simple groups.” [S5:42–44] The number-field manuscript: “The conjecture grew from Kneser's quaternionic simplicity question and Platonov's local-to-global simplicity proposal. Margulis formulated the description by open subgroups of the anisotropic finite local product” [S11:45–48], citing the same 1979 book at the same place — Russian original, Section 2.4.8 [S5:42–45] [S11:45–48]. Both also record that Margulis's own contribution stops short of the theorem: “The finite-index assertion in his Corollary~2.4.9 is an essential first step. The further problem is to show that each resulting finite quotient comes from the specified local groups.” [S5:46–48] The arithmetic framework — strong approximation and the normal subgroup theorem — is credited to the standard sources [S11:49–50].

The division-algebra case is the spine of the published history, and the function-field manuscript narrates it in order. “The quaternion case was completed by Margulis in 1980, as recalled in Tomanov's historical account” [S5:55–57], and “Tomanov later proved the conjecture for division algebras of power-of-two index and reduced arbitrary index to odd index” [S5:57–59]. Then “Rapinchuk--Potapchik reduced the number-field problem to excluding nonabelian finite simple quotients of the multiplicative group of the division algebra” [S5:64–66]; Segev supplied the commuting-graph exclusion and Segev–Seitz verified the required finite-simple-group properties [S5:67–69]; and “Rapinchuk--Segev--Seitz proved the stronger result that every finite quotient of the multiplicative group of a finite-dimensional division algebra is solvable; their Theorem~6.4 proves the Margulis--Platonov conjecture for inner type~$A$ over arbitrary global fields” [S5:70–74], with a shorter proof later from two-generation of finite simple groups [S5:75–76]. The number-field manuscript gives the same chain with the same attributions [S11:60–71] and lists the established cases as surveyed in the literature [S11:72–74], including Chernousov's projective-simplicity theorem for anisotropic groups of absolute rank at least two that split over a quadratic extension [S11:54–57].

The state of the problem immediately before these two manuscripts is stated precisely, and it is a statement about an open residue rather than about the whole conjecture. Over a function field, the anisotropic groups are of type *A* by Harder's theorem [S5:78–80], the isotropic case follows from the global Kneser–Tits theorem [S5:81–82], and the inner type-*A* case is the Rapinchuk–Segev–Seitz theorem, so the residue is anisotropic outer forms of type *A* [S5:83–85]. The manuscript records that the residue was load-bearing elsewhere in the literature: “general outer-type-$A$ applications were still explicitly conditional on it in” a 2026 corollary [S5:86–88]. The number-field manuscript's residue is the same outer-*A* family plus the trialitarian *D*₄ and *E*₆ cases [S11:72–74].

Prior (my training data, not this repo.) The Margulis–Platonov conjecture was, as of my cutoff, a long-standing open problem in the arithmetic theory of algebraic groups, with the inner type-*A* case settled by Rapinchuk–Segev–Seitz and the remaining anisotropic outer forms of type *A*, together with *D*₄ and *E*₆, open; the function-field case was generally treated as accessible relative to the number-field case because Harder's classification narrows the anisotropic groups. A proof covering both field types, with characteristic two included and with abstract normal subgroups and no finite-generation hypothesis, is the kind of result that closes a problem the field had treated as a named open conjecture for decades. That paragraph is my prior, not a repo claim; the repo's own history is the citations above.

## Consequences now that the proof has been found

The family's two manuscripts are a chain, and the chain is recorded inside the corpus. The function-field manuscript's bibliography opens with an entry keyed `MPNF` whose title is “The Margulis--Platonov conjecture over number fields” [S7:1–7] — the number-field manuscript of this same family, cited by its release href — and its introduction names it as “The immediate predecessor is the number-field theorem of” that entry [S5:94–97]. The dependency is one-way: the number-field manuscript's bibliography is 293 lines and contains no entry for the function-field manuscript and no OpenAI-preprint entry at all [S13], which is consistent with its earlier date. So within family 018 the October manuscript builds on the September one, and the number-field result is an input to the function-field proof rather than a consequence of it.

The joint consequence is the release's own headline: the conjecture over every global field. The family entry states it as “Proves the Margulis--Platonov conjecture over every global field, including function fields of characteristic two.” [S2:112] Neither manuscript proves that alone; the number-field manuscript proves the number-field case [S11:31–34] and the function-field manuscript proves the function-field case [S5:18–27], and the union is the family claim. The chain matters for how strong that claim is: the function-field half rests on the number-field half, so the pair is only as strong as the number-field proof, and neither half is machine-checked.

Two consequences are stated inside the manuscripts. The number-field manuscript derives the positive-density congruence subgroup property for every absolutely almost simple simply connected group over a number field, as an application of its theorem to a theorem that assumes the conjecture [S11:95–117] — a conditional-input consequence, with its range explicitly bounded [S11:128–131]. The function-field manuscript's consequence is a closure of a conditional dependency in the literature: it supplies the normal-subgroup description for the anisotropic outer type-*A* groups in every characteristic [S5:89–90], the case that a 2026 application had left conditional [S5:86–88].

What is not a consequence: the congruence subgroup problem in general, Serre's finite-*S* conjecture, statements about arbitrary infinite *S*, centrality or finiteness of congruence kernels outside the positive-density range, and any claim about groups that are not absolutely almost simple and simply connected. The family also proves nothing about the isotropic cases, which it takes from the Kneser–Tits theorem and the known inner type-*A* theorem [S6:4–7] [S11:75–76].
